# 1. RESEARCH PROBLEM

Privacy-preserving RAG systems generally use predetermined privacy mechanisms or fixed privacy configurations.

A fixed configuration cannot explicitly adapt its privacy–utility–latency trade-off according to the privacy characteristics of an individual input.

The proposed system therefore follows:

**Privacy Evidence Detection
→ Robustness and Reliability Estimation
→ Privacy Risk Estimation
→ Adaptive Privacy Decision
→ Privacy Enforcement
→ Local RAG
→ Explainability, Evaluation and Deployment**

The central research hypothesis is:

> An adaptive privacy framework that selects protection according to input-specific privacy evidence can achieve a better privacy–utility–latency trade-off than fixed privacy configurations.

This is a hypothesis to be experimentally tested, not assumed to be true.

---

# 2. FUNDAMENTAL DESIGN PRINCIPLE

Deep learning is **NOT the privacy mechanism**.

Deep learning provides privacy intelligence.

The system must distinguish:

### Privacy detection

What privacy-sensitive information is present?

### Robustness

Will the detector still identify that information when it is obfuscated or adversarially modified?

### Reliability

How trustworthy is the detector's prediction?

### Privacy risk

What privacy concern results from the detected evidence?

### Adaptive decision

What protection configuration should be used for this input?

### Enforcement

Actually transform/protect sensitive information according to the selected configuration.

### RAG

Retrieve relevant local information and generate a response using privacy-protected context.

### Evaluation

Determine whether adaptation actually improves privacy, utility and latency.

### Explainability/deployment

Explain and record the complete privacy decision and provide an integrated executable system.

These responsibilities must remain separated even if internal methodologies later change.

# 3. WHY THE PREVIOUS ARCHITECTURE WAS CHANGED

The previous design used:

Raw multi-domain QA datasets
→ rule/NER evidence
→ Snorkel weak supervision
→ multi-task DeBERTa predictions
→ uncertainty
→ Dempster–Shafer risk
→ privacy policy.

The multi-task targets included concepts such as:

* sensitivity;
* intent;
* threat;
* disclosure/risk-related targets.

This created a methodological weakness because the downstream neural model was learning labels that were themselves generated from project-defined rules.

Real experiments also showed severe weak-supervision limitations, including approximately 49–66% all-abstain behavior for several targets and some classes receiving no Snorkel argmax predictions.

The revised architecture therefore makes one major correction:

> Use externally annotated typed PII supervision for the primary privacy-detection task instead of manufacturing the primary labels through Snorkel.

Consequently:

* Snorkel is removed as the primary supervision mechanism.
* The old multi-head sensitivity/intent/threat architecture is removed.
* DeBERTa is trained directly for typed privacy-entity detection.
* Privacy risk is handled downstream rather than pretending that high-level risk labels are externally available.
* Privacy enforcement and local RAG are explicitly implemented so that the privacy decision has an observable system consequence.

## 4.3 Public/non-private RAG control corpus

A public QA corpus such as Natural Questions or HotpotQA may be retained for RAG/control evaluation if needed.

Its purpose is not to manufacture privacy labels.

# 7. M2 — PRIVACY ENTITY DETECTION

## 7.1 Purpose

Train a deep-learning model to detect typed privacy-sensitive entities directly from externally annotated PII spans.

M2 answers:

> Which privacy-sensitive entities occur in this text, where are they located, what type are they, and how confident is the detector?

---

## 7.2 Input

Text:

x = (w1, w2, ..., wn)

with training-time typed entity spans.

---

## 7.3 Output

For inference:

E(x) = {(s_i, e_i, t_i, p_i)}

where:

* s_i = entity start;
* e_i = entity end;
* t_i = predicted PII type;
* p_i = prediction confidence.

---

## 7.4 Primary methodology

DeBERTa-based token classification is the primary current methodology.

Use BIO/BIO-compatible sequence labeling as required by the selected benchmark representation.

Do not claim DeBERTa is universally state-of-the-art.

---

## 7.5 Model comparison

At least one lighter transformer baseline must be supported.

The purpose is to test:

> Does the detection improvement from DeBERTa justify its additional inference cost?

Compare:

* detection F1;
* recall;
* rare-type performance;
* latency;
* memory/compute requirements.

---

## 7.6 Loss

Support:

* standard cross entropy;
* weighted cross entropy.

Focal loss may be added as an experimental alternative if justified.

Do not rebalance the entire dataset artificially without evidence.

---

## 7.7 Training

Required:

* tokenization;
* token/span alignment;
* dynamic label mapping;
* attention masks;
* loss masks;
* training loop;
* validation loop;
* early stopping;
* checkpointing;
* resume support;
* best-model selection.

Hyperparameters must be configurable.

## 7.8 Sequence length

Run a coverage analysis for candidate lengths such as:

Measure:

* percentage of records truncated;

# 8. M3 — ADVERSARIAL PRIVACY ROBUSTNESS LEARNING

## 8.1 Purpose

Improve the robustness of M2 against adversarial perturbation and privacy-entity obfuscation.

M3 answers:

> Does the privacy detector continue detecting sensitive entities when their representation is deliberately altered?

---

## 8.2 Locked Review-2 methodology

FGSM is locked as the adversarial-training methodology for Review 2.

Do not replace FGSM with FreeLB or another method unless explicitly instructed after Review 2.

---

## 8.3 Core formulation

For model representation H and loss L:

r_adv = epsilon × sign(gradient_H L)

H_adv = H + r_adv

Training should combine clean and adversarial objectives.

Conceptually:

L_total = L_clean + lambda_adv L_adv

where epsilon and lambda_adv are configurable and selected using validation evidence.

---

## 8.4 Training flow

M2 checkpoint
→ clean forward pass
→ gradient computation
→ FGSM perturbation
→ adversarial forward pass
→ combined loss
→ optimizer update
→ validation
→ robust checkpoint.

---

## 8.5 Realistic textual robustness evaluation

FGSM alone is insufficient as a real-world robustness evaluation.

Create label-preserving textual perturbations including examples such as:

* character substitution;
* separator insertion;
* whitespace modification;
* symbol replacement;
* email obfuscation;
* phone-number formatting;
* digit-to-word conversion;
* case modification;
* token splitting.

Example:

[john.doe@gmail.com](mailto:john.doe@gmail.com)

may become a semantically equivalent obfuscated representation.

The attack must not change the underlying privacy label.

---

## 8.6 Required comparison

Compare:

M2 clean detector

versus

M3 FGSM-hardened detector.

Evaluate both on:

* clean data;
* adversarial/obfuscated data.

---

## 8.7 Metrics

Include:

* clean F1;
* adversarial F1;
* robustness degradation;
* attack success rate where meaningful;
* clean-performance degradation;
* training overhead;
* inference overhead.

M3 survives as a research module only if its robustness role can be empirically demonstrated.

# 9. M4 — PRIVACY UNCERTAINTY QUANTIFICATION AND CALIBRATION

## 9.1 Purpose

Estimate how reliable the privacy detector's predictions are before those predictions influence risk and policy decisions.

M4 answers:

> How much should the adaptive system trust the detector's confidence for this input?

---

## 9.2 Current candidate methodologies

Two distinct techniques are currently implemented/evaluated:

### Temperature Scaling

Used for confidence calibration.

### MC Dropout

Used for predictive uncertainty approximation.

These are not conceptually identical.

---

## 9.3 Temperature scaling

Given logits z:

p = softmax(z / T)

Fit positive temperature T on calibration data only:

T* = argmin_T NLL(D_cal)

Do not update detector weights during temperature fitting.

---

## 9.4 MC Dropout

Perform multiple stochastic forward passes with dropout active.

For T_MC passes:

p_bar = (1 / T_MC) Σ p_t

Estimate uncertainty using appropriate predictive statistics such as entropy/variation.

---

## 9.5 MC Dropout is NOT permanently guaranteed

This module must explicitly compare:

A. raw softmax;

B. temperature scaling;

C. temperature scaling + MC Dropout.

Measure whether MC Dropout contributes useful error discrimination beyond calibrated confidence.

If MC Dropout adds large latency without useful downstream information, it may be removed after Review 2 without changing the architecture.

Therefore downstream modules must consume a generic reliability/uncertainty interface rather than depend directly on MC Dropout internals.

---

## 9.6 MC-pass experiment

Candidate stochastic pass counts may include:

5
10
20
30
50

Measure:

* uncertainty convergence;
* prediction stability;
* error discrimination;
* latency.

Do not select 30 or 50 merely because another implementation used it.

---

## 9.7 Metrics

Include:

* Expected Calibration Error;
* Negative Log-Likelihood;
* Brier score where appropriate;
* reliability diagram;
* error-detection performance;
* risk-coverage behavior;
* latency.

---

## 9.8 Output

M4 should provide:

* calibrated confidence;
* uncertainty score;
* calibration metadata;
* reliability statistics.

# 10. M5 — PRIVACY RISK AGGREGATION

## 10.1 Purpose

Convert entity-level privacy evidence into an input/context-level privacy-risk representation required by adaptive policy selection.

M5 answers:

> Given the detected sensitive entities and the reliability of those detections, what privacy risk should the policy engine consider?

---

## 10.2 Current Review-2 methodology

Dempster–Shafer Evidence Theory is the primary current candidate.

However, DS is NOT assumed to be superior.

The final formal module name must remain undecided until manual review/experimental comparison.

---

## 10.3 Inputs

Potential evidence includes:

* detected PII types;
* number of entities;
* entity-type diversity;
* calibrated confidence;
* uncertainty;
* entity combinations;
* observable context features.

Avoid counting multiple highly correlated values from the same model as independent evidence without justification.

---

## 10.4 Dempster–Shafer representation

For frame of discernment:

Theta_R = {Low, Medium, High, Critical}

define mass function:

m: 2^Theta_R -> [0,1]

with:

m(empty) = 0

and:

Σ m(A) = 1.

Support:

* evidence masses;
* Dempster combination;
* conflict coefficient;
* belief;
* plausibility;
* pignistic probability where required.

---

## 10.5 Critical restriction

DO NOT create unexplained mappings such as:

EMAIL -> 0.7 Medium

BANK_ACCOUNT -> 0.9 Critical

and describe those numbers as ground truth.

Mass construction must be:

* explicit;
* configurable;
* traceable;
* replaceable;
* experimentally evaluated.

If external standards/literature support category ordering or policy significance, store those mappings separately from the DS fusion algorithm.

---

## 10.6 Mandatory aggregation comparison

Implement a common risk aggregation interface and at least three methods:

### A. Maximum-risk baseline

Use the strongest detected evidence.

### B. Transparent weighted aggregation baseline

Use an explicitly documented weighted aggregation rule.

### C. Dempster–Shafer aggregation

Use evidence-theoretic combination.

The architecture must permit all three without changing downstream M6.

---

## 10.7 Evaluation

Compare methods using:

* stability;
* conflict handling;
* sensitivity analysis;
* runtime;
* downstream policy behavior;
* end-to-end privacy/utility results when available.

Do not claim DS is superior until the experiment supports it.

---

# 11. M6 — ADAPTIVE PRIVACY POLICY SELECTION

## 11.1 Purpose

M6 is the central adaptive decision module.

It answers:

> Given the privacy risk and reliability of the evidence, what privacy protection configuration should be applied to this input?

M6 must be substantial.

It must NOT be implemented as only:

if risk == LOW: P1
if risk == MEDIUM: P2
if risk == HIGH: P3
if risk == CRITICAL: P4.

---

## 11.2 Policy space

Define:

Π = {π1, π2, ..., πK}

Each policy must represent executable privacy parameters.

A policy may include:

* epsilon;
* perturbation strength;
* protected entity categories;
* entity coverage;
* mechanism selection;
* fallback behavior;
* other M7 parameters.

Do not copy the previous cloud-oriented P1–P4 definitions containing IPFE/OT/LSH unless those mechanisms are actually implemented.

---

## 11.3 Current optimization concept

The current preferred formulation is constrained policy optimization.

For every policy π, maintain/estimate:

P(π) = privacy protection characteristic

U(π) = utility characteristic

L(π) = latency/overhead characteristic.

For input x, M5 and M4 provide the evidence needed to determine required protection:

P_required(x).

Conceptually select:

π* = argmin L(π)

subject to:

P(π) >= P_required(x)

and:

U(π) >= U_min.

This means:

> Select the lowest-overhead policy that satisfies the required privacy protection while preserving acceptable utility.

---

## 11.4 Why not arbitrary weighted optimization

Avoid immediately defining:

J = alpha P + beta U + gamma L

with arbitrary values such as:

alpha = 0.5
beta = 0.3
gamma = 0.2.

Such values create unnecessary methodological questions unless experimentally justified.

---

## 11.5 Uncertainty handling

M4 reliability information may make policy selection more conservative.

Example concept:

higher uncertainty
→ less confidence in detected privacy evidence
→ stronger fallback/protection requirement.

The exact mathematical relationship must remain configurable and testable.

---

## 11.6 Required subfunctions

M6 implementation should include:

* policy repository;
* policy schema;
* policy candidate generator;
* policy profiler;
* privacy constraint evaluator;
* utility constraint evaluator;
* latency/cost evaluator;
* feasibility checker;
* policy optimizer;
* uncertainty-aware adjustment;
* fallback selector;
* fixed-policy baseline selector;
* decision logger;
* sensitivity-analysis engine.

This ensures M6 is a genuine algorithmic module rather than a mapping table.

---

## 11.7 Evaluation

M6 must eventually be compared against:

* no privacy;
* fixed weak privacy;
* fixed strong privacy;
* proposed adaptive privacy.

Policy characteristics must be tuned/profiled using development/validation experiments, never final test results.

# 12. M7 — ADAPTIVE PRIVACY ENFORCEMENT

## 12.1 Purpose

M7 converts the abstract policy selected by M6 into actual privacy protection.

M7 answers:

> How should the sensitive entities be transformed according to the selected privacy policy?

---

## 12.2 Current methodology

Use entity-level Local Differential Privacy inspired by LPRAG.

The mechanism family includes:

* Word Perturbation Mechanism (WPM);
* Number Perturbation Mechanism (NPM);
* Phrase Perturbation Mechanism (PPM);
* adaptive privacy-budget allocation.

---

## 12.3 Policy interface

Input:

* sensitive entity;
* entity type;
* selected policy;
* epsilon;
* coverage/protection settings.

Output:

* protected entity/context;
* applied mechanism;
* privacy parameters;
* audit information.

---

## 12.4 Adaptive epsilon

The system must NOT pretend that changing epsilon dynamically can retroactively alter a static pre-perturbed vector index.

The current local-RAG implementation should therefore keep privacy enforcement modular and support policy-conditioned perturbation of sensitive retrieved context before generation.

This is an adaptation of LPRAG-style mechanisms to the local RAG threat model.

Do not claim that the complete original LPRAG privacy architecture is reproduced unchanged.

---

## 12.5 WPM

Word-like private entities should use the selected word perturbation mechanism.

Implementation should include:

* candidate generation;
* semantic similarity handling where required;
* randomized selection;
* epsilon-controlled probability;
* reproducibility controls.

---

## 12.6 NPM

Numeric entities require number-aware perturbation rather than inappropriate word substitution.

Support the selected numeric perturbation mechanism according to the source methodology.

---

## 12.7 PPM

Multi-token phrases require phrase-aware protection and privacy-budget composition.

Do not independently perturb components in a way that violates the intended privacy accounting.

---

## 12.8 Required implementation

Include:

* policy parser;
* entity-type router;
* mechanism router;
* privacy-budget allocator;
* WPM;
* NPM;
* PPM;
* epsilon validation;

## 12.9 Evaluation

* semantic preservation;
* perturbation success;

* latency.

# 31. FINAL REVIEW-2 RESEARCH STORY

The project should be explainable in one coherent sequence:

> Externally annotated privacy data is integrated and validated. A DeBERTa-based model learns typed PII detection. FGSM adversarial training improves detector robustness. Calibration and uncertainty estimation quantify prediction reliability. Entity-level evidence is aggregated into privacy risk. An adaptive policy module selects an appropriate protection configuration. Entity-level LDP mechanisms enforce the selected policy on sensitive context. A local RAG pipeline retrieves information and generates responses using protected context. Finally, the system evaluates the privacy–utility–latency trade-off and provides explainable decision analytics, audit information and deployment integration.

The project contribution is NOT any one of these algorithms in isolation.

The thesis contribution is the **adaptive composition of privacy intelligence, risk-aware policy selection and enforceable privacy protection inside an evaluated RAG pipeline**, together with experimental evidence comparing adaptive privacy against fixed privacy configurations.
